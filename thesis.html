THE THESIS

Commerce doesn't start at the transaction.
It starts with a decision.

The product lives on the other page. This one is the argument underneath it — who gets credit for taste, what the graph learns from a night, and what that turns out to be worth to the rooms on the other side of it.

01 — THE ANCHOR

Every night out has a table in the middle of it.

Dinner is where a night gets committed. It's the first thing you lock, and everything else is timed around it — the car before, the show after, who's actually coming. So that's where we build from: the reservation book, and the restaurant floor.

THE BOOK Resy Where the table gets held

A night isn't real until someone has the reservation. The book is where intent stops being a group chat and becomes a commitment with a time on it — which makes it the cleanest signal of what a night is actually going to be, and the first thing a Playlist has to reach to be executable rather than a suggestion.

THE FLOOR Toast Where the night gets paid

The book says a table was promised. The floor says what actually happened — what they ordered, what they spent, how long they stayed, whether they came back. That's the outcome half of the loop, and without it a recommendation never learns whether it produced a good night or just a booked one.

Food and beverage is the substrate

Concerts are occasional. Dinner is weekly. If you want to learn how groups actually decide, you have to sit where the frequent decision happens — not the once-a-quarter one.

Two halves nobody holds together

The book and the floor sit in different systems, and neither is joined to the ticket, the car or the group that came. Joining them is the whole product.

Everything else hangs off that spine

Once dinner is anchored and timed, tickets, transport and the split are arithmetic. Start anywhere else and you're guessing at the middle of the night.

02 — THE BEHAVIOR

Going out stopped being an occasion.

For most people under forty, eating out isn't the special version of the week — it's the week. Dinner is how you see anyone. The bar after is where you actually catch up. A show is just the excuse to get eight people in the same place. It's several of these decisions every week, made fast, in a group chat, with real money attached and nobody appointed to decide.

FREQUENT, NOT SPECIAL The planning cost is paid weekly

An annual anniversary dinner is worth an hour of research. Thursday isn't — and Thursday is the one that keeps coming. Repeat a small tax two or three times a week and it stops being small; it becomes the reason people default to the same four places.

SOCIAL BEFORE CULINARY The room matters more than the menu

Nobody picks a place purely on the food. They pick on who's coming, how loud it is, whether you can stay, and what's within walking distance after. A rating of the cooking keeps answering a question nobody asked.

SPENT, NOT SAVED It's a real line in the budget

Going out is one of the largest discretionary things this group spends on, and the number is set by a plan nobody wrote down — decided in fragments, across four apps, and only fully known once it's already gone.

So the customer isn't someone hunting for a restaurant. It's someone who is going out on Friday either way — and would like it to be good, to work, and to cost roughly what they expected.

03 — THE ROOT OF IT

Everyone has that one friend who always knows a place.

That person is doing unpaid A&R for an entire city.

They found the room in month one, not month nine when it's already impossible to book. They know which places quietly stopped being good and never say so publicly. When they tell you to go, you go. And none of it is worth anything to them, because nothing anywhere records that they were first.

Music solved this decades ago. A&R is a real job with a real title: find it early, back it, and get credited when it works. Restaurants and live experiences have exactly the same function running inside every friend group in every city — and none of the infrastructure.

THEY'RE EARLY First, not loudest

Being right about a room everyone already knows is worth nothing. The value sits entirely with the person who was there before the line, and the only proof of that is a timestamp nobody currently keeps.

THEY'RE RIGHT REPEATEDLY A record, not a hit

Anyone is right once. The real signal is the person whose calls keep landing — across years, across neighbourhoods, across completely different kinds of night. That's a track record, and it can be counted.

THEY MOVE BEHAVIOUR People change their Friday

Not likes, not saves. Six people actually went somewhere else because of one sentence they said in a group chat — which becomes measurable the instant the booking is attached to the recommendation.

AND THEY GET NOTHING "I told you about that place"

The room gets the covers. The platform gets the data. The person who caused all of it gets to say one sentence at a table, which is the entire compensation structure of taste as it currently exists.

07 — UNDER THE HOOD

The matching engine, and how it gets cold-started.

The hard part was never finding a restaurant. It's solving a constraint problem across a group of people in real time — and the taste layer only narrows the candidate set. Turning that set into an evening that's sequenced, priced, held and split is the agentic part, and it's where most recommendation products stop.

01 · READ Intent → constraints

One sentence becomes a solvable spec: who's coming, where each of them starts, the ceiling per person, the hard nos, and what time it has to end.

02 · CANDIDATES Weighted by behavior

Rooms that fit the group, ranked by who in your circle actually went back — and by which pairings have produced good nights before, not by who paid.

03 · SEQUENCE Solve the order

Travel time between rooms, kitchen close, set time, last call. This is where recommendations die — they return places when what you needed was an evening.

04 · HOLD Against live inventory

Check what's genuinely available, hold each piece at once, and price the whole thing per person before anyone is asked to commit.

05 · SETTLE Book, split, learn

Confirm everything, charge each person their share, then record how the night actually went — which is the only input that makes the next match better.

COLD START · SUPPLY

Index the rooms the internet already argues about.

A graph with nothing in it recommends nothing. So the first supply indexed is the set of restaurants already generating public signal — a waitlist, a reservation drop people set alarms for, a permanent spot on every list, a comment section doing the work of a focus group. That demand doesn't have to be manufactured; it's already legible from outside.

Visibility is a free demand signal These rooms are the easiest in the city to be right about, because the whole internet is already telling you they're wanted.
They're also the hardest to get into An agent is worth most exactly where a table is scarce. "It got us in" is a reason to keep using something; "it found a place" isn't.
One hard room anchors a whole night Lock the scarce thing first and the rest of the evening — timing, transport, what comes after — resolves around it. Supply density can then grow outward from the anchor.
COLD START · TASTE

Food creators are already doing this job, in a worse format.

There is a large, working, mostly unpaid recommendation network already running: people who go out constantly, post it, and move real traffic with no way to prove it. Today their output is a video and a saved link. The room they sent two hundred people to gets the covers; they get a follower count. A Playlist is the same output as an executable object with their name on it.

The taste supply is also the distribution The people who populate the graph are the same people who fill the rooms. Seeding content and acquiring users is one motion here, not two budgets.
Attribution they can't currently get Nobody can presently prove a post caused a booking. Provenance can — which is a materially better deal than a flat fee and a discount code.
The market scores them honestly Being repeatedly right becomes measurable, so influence stops being audience size and becomes a track record. That's a ranking a brand can buy against and a diner can trust.

11 — DISTRIBUTION

Three surfaces already hold the pieces. None of them hold the night.

Nobody is waiting for another app to check. The opening is that the intent, the inventory and the taste already live in products this audience opens every day — they just live in three different ones, and not one of them can finish an evening.

HAS THE INTENT Raya Creates the night, then abandons it

A dating product manufactures the highest-stakes planning problem there is and then stops at "so where should we go?" Date night finishes that sentence — staged, priced, held, and able to end gracefully without either person having to say it out loud. For a network that sells taste, an evening that reflects well on both people is the product.

THE WEDGE The moment after the match. They keep the relationship; we handle the night.
HAS THE INVENTORY Resy One hour of a five-hour night

The book knows a table was wanted. It doesn't know about the ticket before it, the room after it, or the four people splitting the cost. Wrapping the reservation in the rest of the evening raises what a single cover is worth to the restaurant, and gives the book a reason to exist at hours other than 7:30.

THE WEDGE Everything on either side of the reservation — which nobody currently sells.
HAS THE TASTE TikTok Where the recommendation dead-ends

This is where discovery actually happens now, and it terminates completely. A video makes you want a place and then hands you nothing — no table, no time, no group, no price. Every food video is a Playlist that was never made executable. The creator gets views; the room gets walk-ins it can't attribute to anybody.

WHO UNDERWRITES IT

CPG budgets are already paying these creators — for views.

Beauty, beverage and snack brands spend heavily against exactly this cohort, through exactly these creators, and what they receive back is reach. Point the same budget at an authored night and it buys booked covers with names and a provenance chain attached. That's the backstop: the money is already committed to this audience — it's just currently spent on impressions.

No new budget line

Influencer and experiential spend against urban 21–40s is already committed and already renewing. This competes for existing dollars on better reporting, which is a far shorter sale than inventing a category.

One relationship, three jobs

The same creator is the taste that populates the graph, the distribution that fills the rooms, and the brand's existing paid partner. Signing one person does the work of three separate acquisition motions.

It prices better in the room

A CPM against an unverified audience, or a cost per booked cover with the chain that produced it. Only one of those survives a marketing review that's being asked to prove incrementality.

Same audience as the pilot

Dense urban 21–40s who go out weekly. The brands want them, the creators reach them, and New York, Los Angeles and Miami are where they already are — so supply, demand and spend converge in the same three markets.

12 — THE STACK

It sits on top of what restaurants and venues already run.

Nobody is ripping out their POS or their book. The systems underneath already hold the tables, the covers, the tickets and the cars — what none of them holds is the person deciding, the four people coming with them, and the reason tonight is happening at all. That's the layer, and this is where it sits.

Additive, not a rip-and-replace

A venue keeps its book, its POS and its ticketing. We arrive as demand with a name, a party size and a card already attached — which is the one thing every operator wants more of.

Who, where, when and why

A book knows a table was wanted at 8. It doesn't know who else is coming, where they're travelling from, what they're doing after, or why tonight matters. Matching on all four is what turns a cover into an evening.

The same engine, both ends of the market

The hardest ticket in the city and a quiet Tuesday counter are the same problem with different scarcity. One matcher serves the sold-out show and the room that needs filling on a Wednesday.

The loop is the moat

Outcomes come back up. Who returned, who brought someone, which pairing worked — that flows into the graph and makes the next match better. The systems underneath see their own slice; only this layer sees the sequence.
